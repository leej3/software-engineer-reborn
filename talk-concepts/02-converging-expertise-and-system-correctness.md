# Converging Expertise and System Correctness

AI causes two previously separate forms of expertise to converge: knowing what outcome matters and knowing how to construct a system that produces it reliably.

A director, operator, product leader, or domain expert may be better prepared to define the destination.
A software engineer may be better prepared to determine whether an apparent solution is a dependable system.
Neither form of expertise is sufficient alone.

This suggests two complementary kinds of correctness:

- **Outcome correctness:** Did we solve the right problem?
  Did anything valuable happen in the real world?
- **System correctness:** Can we explain, reproduce, observe, scale, secure, maintain, and govern the behavior?

Domain leaders often begin with an advantage in outcome correctness.
Software engineers often begin with an advantage in system correctness.
AI lowers the cost for both groups to cross into the other's territory, causing several professions to converge on a role that combines their strengths.

## Where Software Enters

Software is more than the code used to implement an idea.
It is the structure that converts intention into repeatable machinery.

> A prompt can produce an outcome.
> A software system makes that outcome
> observable, repeatable, governable, and scalable.

An AI-generated prototype may produce the desired result once.
A dependable system must also answer questions about state, changing dependencies, permissions, failure, load, evaluation, versioning, review, rollback, and accountability.
AI does not remove these questions.
Its probabilistic and persuasive behavior can make them more important.

Software engineers therefore have a meaningful advantage: experience imposing structure on behavior and making failures visible.
Their advantage is not privileged knowledge of syntax, but familiarity with the transition from "it worked" to "it is a working system."

## Professions Converging on a New Role

This transition is not an automatic victory for software engineers.
They can lose it if they cling to implementation as their identity.
As implementation becomes cheaper, they must expand toward domain judgment, communication, and ownership of real-world outcomes.

Domain experts, directors, and project or product leaders face the complementary challenge.
They can increasingly construct systems directly, but must learn enough about systems, evidence, and failure not to be deceived by a persuasive prototype.

The emerging role closes the entire loop:

1. Understand which outcome matters.
2. Specify what success means in observable terms.
3. Construct a system that can produce the outcome.
4. Evaluate its behavior and expose its failures.
5. Improve it through controlled feedback.
6. Remain accountable for the result.

The reborn software engineer is one candidate for this role, not its guaranteed owner.
Software engineers have a valuable head start in making unreliable components dependable, but they must learn to identify what matters beyond the system.
Domain experts have a head start in defining meaningful outcomes, but they must learn how dependable systems are distinguished from convincing demonstrations.

This convergence creates a constructive goal for both: combine outcome judgment with systems judgment, and take responsibility for the complete path between intention and consequence.
