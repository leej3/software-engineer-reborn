# Production, Judgment, and Ownership

AI makes it cheap to produce plausible work.
It allows us to attempt work well beyond the boundaries of our established expertise, but it does not automatically expand our ability to judge that work at the same rate.

This creates a central problem for human–AI collaboration:

> AI expands the territory in which we can produce faster than it expands the
> territory in which we can reliably judge.

Expertise provides an anchor: a domain in which we can independently recognize whether a result is correct, useful, or misleading.
AI can then help us become operationally useful in adjacent fields without pretending that we have mastered them.

Feedback allows us to move farther from that anchor.
Tests, measurements, executable results, user observation, peer review, and bounded milestones give reality opportunities to contradict both the AI and our own assumptions.

Ownership remains human.
Someone must define the objective, decide what evidence is sufficient, and accept responsibility for acting on the result.
Producing an artifact is not the same as understanding or owning its consequences.

## Why This Matters for Software Engineers

Software engineers are not automatically experts or good judges.
However, the practice of software engineering can cultivate useful habits for this problem: decomposing ambiguous work, making assumptions explicit, constructing feedback loops, investigating failures, and integrating imperfect components into a system that must work in reality.

The opportunity is to apply those habits beyond writing code.
As code production becomes less scarce, the engineer's value may increasingly lie in directing work, making its failures visible, judging its fitness for purpose, and remaining accountable for the outcome.
