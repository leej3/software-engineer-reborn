# Candidate C: Producing work and standing behind it

Public talk title: The Software Engineer Reborn: A Vision of Human–AI Collaboration.
Candidate subtitle: What changes when I can produce more than I can readily explain?

Argument: AI expands production while changing how we assess work, take responsibility, and experience expertise.
Sequence: a personal difficulty → brief context → two assessment patterns → demonstration → implications for work and identity.
Total: 50 minutes across 13 slides.
First-person language is proposed wording for review.

## Slide 1

Slide number: 1

Slide title: I had a working result, but could not readily explain it

Slide content description: Show the three elements of the source's meeting example: functioning artifact; a reasonable question; returning to the code to find the answer.

Slide background/comments: Tell the account plainly, without inventing dialogue or turning it into a professional crisis. Explain why this felt different from personally constructing a solution and retaining its reasoning. The experience establishes the question the audience will follow: what would it take to stand behind the result? Source: [professional identity](../talk-concepts/03-rebuilding-professional-identity.md).
Transition: Establish why John accepts this difficulty as part of a consequential change in capability.

Time: 4 minutes; elapsed: 4 minutes.

## Slide 2

Slide number: 2

Slide title: Why I still think AI is transformative

Slide content description: One personal example of crossing technical boundaries, then the broader possibilities for individuals and repeated organizational work.
Label the societal conclusion as John's view.

Slide background/comments: Use the car-comparison dashboard or another existing artifact described in [professional identity](../talk-concepts/03-rebuilding-professional-identity.md).
Briefly explain the range of work it made feasible.
Avoid claiming that the meeting example cancels the gain or that the gain settles all questions of productivity.
Transition: Capability also makes consequences and resource use relevant.

Time: 2 minutes; elapsed: 6 minutes.

## Slide 3

Slide number: 3

Slide title: Useful capabilities come with consequential choices

Slide content description: Possibilities for useful work; misuse and resource costs; distribution of the benefits.
Include one cited energy example.

Slide background/comments: Use [energy evidence](evidence-notes.md#energy-and-resource-costs).
This ends the broad societal context after two slides.
Keep security report details, water references, and investment scenarios in discussion notes unless specifically selected later.
Transition: Return to a decision John faces directly: accepting a particular piece of work.

Time: 2 minutes; elapsed: 8 minutes.

## Slide 4

Slide number: 4

Slide title: Quality and ownership are different questions

Slide content description: “Quality ≠ ownership.”
Place “meets its requirements” beside “I can explain, caveat, and stand behind it.”
Add a third question: What evidence do I have?

Slide background/comments: An artifact can improve while the producer's understanding weakens. Quality concerns the result; evidence supports confidence; ownership here means the practical relationship to responsibility and control, not a legal claim about copyright. Do not equate confidence with correctness. Source: [assessing quality](../talk-concepts/10-assessing-quality.md) and John's latest notes.
Transition: The kind of evidence depends on whether we assess an artifact or repeated system behavior.

Time: 4 minutes; elapsed: 12 minutes.

## Slide 5

Slide number: 5

Slide title: Pattern one: an expert produces a particular artifact

Slide content description: An illustrative car-comparison report with its requirements, data sources, calculations, and unresolved assumptions visible beside it.

Slide background/comments: Explain how AI can help produce a report, analysis, design, or software change whose fitness still requires judgment. Evidence should make assessment less expensive than reconstructing the work from scratch. A polished appearance or a large generated explanation does not establish quality. Use “easy to assess” before introducing the word assessability. Source: [assessing quality](../talk-concepts/10-assessing-quality.md).
Transition: Explain what changes if this becomes a service used repeatedly.

Time: 4 minutes; elapsed: 16 minutes.

## Slide 6

Slide number: 6

Slide title: Pattern two: a system performs a class of tasks

Slide content description: A hypothetical service that repeatedly produces comparison reports.
Show varied inputs, representative evaluation cases, operating limits, monitoring, and escalation.

Slide background/comments: The question is whether behavior is sufficiently reliable across its intended cases, including failures and changing conditions. Explain the engineering work of bounding capability and evaluating costs and error rates. The service is an illustrative extension, not a claim that John deployed it. Both patterns need expert judgment; system evaluation does not prove every future output correct. Source: [assessing quality](../talk-concepts/10-assessing-quality.md).
Transition: Return to the first pattern for a concrete example of John's working method.

Time: 4 minutes; elapsed: 20 minutes.

## Slide 7

Slide number: 7

Slide title: Demo: from a requested change to an acceptance decision

Slide content description: A Codex workspace, a bounded change, an observable result, a version diff, and the check that matters to the change.

Slide background/comments: Proposed eight-minute demo: one minute on purpose and permission boundaries; two on initiating or replaying the change; three on evidence; two on acceptance and what remains uncertain. Use a simple view with a missing-data case so that the audience can understand both success and a limitation. Show how artifacts and version control help recover what happened. Use a preserved completed example if the live run stalls and identify it clearly. Source: [development environment](../talk-concepts/06-the-ai-era-development-environment.md).
Transition: Ask what the person must retain after closing this task and moving to another.

Time: 8 minutes; elapsed: 28 minutes.

## Slide 8

Slide number: 8

Slide title: What must survive when we move on?

Slide content description: A short handoff containing purpose, decisions, evidence, and open questions.
Beside it, show the underlying artifacts and history that let someone check the summary.

Slide background/comments: Explain compression as a shared problem for the human and the agent. Summaries preserve direction only if consequential details remain discoverable. Several projects and parallel streams make this harder because John must repeatedly recover context. Mention project instructions and skills as ways to carry reusable context. Reserve cache behavior, model selection, and local/cloud settings for discussion unless a visible demo action needs explanation.
Sources: [development environment](../talk-concepts/06-the-ai-era-development-environment.md) and [finite human](../talk-concepts/07-the-finite-human-and-the-tireless-producer.md).
Transition: Saved evidence also has to survive contact with another person's questions.

Time: 4 minutes; elapsed: 32 minutes.

## Slide 9

Slide number: 9

Slide title: Other people's judgment still matters

Slide content description: A producer, a domain expert, and a person responsible for operating the result.
For each: one question they can answer or challenge.

Slide background/comments: Use this to explain why greater individual capability still leaves a need for collaborators. An expert may identify an invalid assumption that no amount of generated explanation resolves. Separate who requested a change, who produced it, who reviewed it, and who can authorize its use. Authorship records and digital identity help preserve history; they do not automatically establish endorsement or understanding. Sources: [community](../talk-concepts/05-human-collaboration-and-community.md) and [staying in the loop](../talk-concepts/09-staying-in-the-loop.md).
Transition: These responsibilities change the content and bargaining conditions of a role.

Time: 4 minutes; elapsed: 36 minutes.

## Slide 10

Slide number: 10

Slide title: How the engineering role may change

Slide content description: Directing production, making artifacts assessable, engineering repeatable AI behavior, and maintaining systems where direct control matters.
Pair responsibility with the ability to inspect, constrain, and stop work.

Slide background/comments: These are possible combinations of work, not a prediction of new job titles. Include a brief dated labor-market observation from [evidence notes](evidence-notes.md#employment), preserving its limitations. Explain that an employer may change expectations before a title changes. Broader domain and organizational knowledge may help engineers connect the work to a useful outcome. Sources: [converging expertise](../talk-concepts/02-converging-expertise-and-system-correctness.md), [favorable starting position](../talk-concepts/08-a-favorable-starting-position.md), and the latest conversation.
Transition: Having useful skills for a changed role does not mean the person will enjoy that role.

Time: 4 minutes; elapsed: 40 minutes.

## Slide 11

Slide number: 11

Slide title: What this feels like for the person doing it

Slide content description: Broader capability; less direct authorship; changed satisfaction; more decisions and context to absorb.

Slide background/comments: Return to John's account of mastery and exhaustion. Name the difference between role transformation and personal identity explicitly. Do not force a positive resolution: John describes the new satisfaction as an incomplete substitute. This gives the opening meeting example its personal meaning without treating it as a moral failure. Source: [professional identity](../talk-concepts/03-rebuilding-professional-identity.md).
Transition: Reconsidering the work requires asking what one values as well as what one can produce.

Time: 4 minutes; elapsed: 44 minutes.

## Slide 12

Slide number: 12

Slide title: Choosing where to adapt

Slide content description: Evaluate a small experiment against usefulness, confidence in the result, enjoyment, and agency.
Include impact, compensation, lifestyle, and community as factors in a larger career decision.

Slide background/comments: Suggest trying one unfamiliar but bounded task with a peer able to review it. Observe whether the new working method fits and what knowledge still needs development. The recommendation is to investigate while there is time to adjust. Different constraints and stages of life imply different next steps; retired participants can contribute expertise and mentorship. Sources: the latest conversation and [staying in the loop](../talk-concepts/09-staying-in-the-loop.md).
Transition: Return to the opening question about standing behind the work.

Time: 4 minutes; elapsed: 48 minutes.

## Slide 13

Slide number: 13

Slide title: What would let me stand behind this work?

Slide content description: Understanding appropriate to the stakes; evidence that can be checked; access to expertise; authority to act on what is found.
Discussion prompt: Which of these is hardest in your experience?

Slide background/comments: Close with a concrete change in professional practice and John's invitation to reassess its fit.
AI's importance, the practical response, and the human cost have now been developed through one question.
Avoid treating a universal method of AI oversight as settled.

Time: 2 minutes; elapsed: 50 minutes.
